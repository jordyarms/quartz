## Translation

> **A Few Words About the Noosphere**  
> _Vladimir Ivanovich Vernadsky_  
> **1943–1944**
>
> First published in the journal *Advances in Modern Biology* (1944, No. 18, Issue 2, pp. 113–120). This was V. I. Vernadsky’s last publication during his lifetime. The electronic version was prepared based on the edition in the book: *V. I. Vernadsky, Scientific Thought as a Planetary Phenomenon*, edited by A. L. Yanshin, Moscow, "Nauka", 1991.
>
> ### 1. The Context of the War
> 
> We are approaching a decisive moment in the Second World War. It resumed in Europe after a 21-year break in 1939, lasting five years in Western Europe and three years in Eastern Europe. In the Far East, it resumed earlier in 1931 and has been ongoing for 13 years.
> 
> In the history of humanity and the biosphere in general, a war of such power, duration, and intensity is an unprecedented phenomenon.
> 
> Moreover, it was preceded by the First World War (1914–1918), which, though less powerful, was closely connected to it causally.
> 
> In our country, the First World War led to a new, historically unprecedented form of statehood—not only in economic terms but also in terms of national aspirations.
> 
> From the perspective of a naturalist (and I believe from a historian's viewpoint as well), such large-scale historical phenomena can and should be viewed as a unified geological process on Earth—not just a historical one.
> 
> The First World War had a decisive impact on my scientific work. It fundamentally changed my geological worldview.
> 
> During the war, I came to a new understanding of nature in geology—one that was forgotten at the time—centered on geochemistry and biogeochemistry, encompassing both inert and living nature from a unified perspective.
> 
> ### 2. The First World War’s Impact on Science
> 
> I spent the years of the First World War immersed in continuous scientific and creative work and have persisted in the same direction to this day.
> 
> In 1915, 28 years ago, the Russian Academy of Sciences in Petrograd formed an academic "Commission for the Study of Productive Forces" of our country, known as KEPS (of which I was the chairman). It played a notable role during the critical period of the First World War. During the war, it became evident that Imperial Russia lacked precise data on what is now termed strategic resources, and we had to urgently consolidate scattered information and address gaps in our knowledge.
> 
> Approaching geological phenomena geochemically and biogeochemically allows us to view all surrounding nature from the same atomic perspective. Unconsciously for me, this coincided with what now characterizes 20th-century science and distinguishes it from earlier centuries. The 20th century is the century of scientific atomism.
> 
> Throughout these years, wherever I was, I was preoccupied with the idea of geochemical and biogeochemical manifestations in the surrounding nature (in the biosphere). Observing it, I simultaneously directed my reading and reflections systematically in this direction.
> 
> ### 3. Humanity and the Biosphere
> 
> In the intensity and complexity of modern life, people often forget that they and all of humanity are inseparably linked to the biosphere—a specific part of the planet where they live. They are geologically connected to its material and energetic structure.
> 
> Typically, humanity is viewed as independent individuals freely constructing their history on Earth. Historians and social scientists often disregard the laws of the biosphere, the Earth's envelope where life can exist. This indivisibility is only now becoming clear.
> 
> No living organism exists freely on Earth; all are inextricably and continuously linked—primarily through nutrition and respiration—to their material-energy environment. Outside this environment, they cannot survive.
> 
> The eminent St. Petersburg academician Kaspar Wolff (1733–1794) vividly expressed this in his 1789 book *On the Particular and Effective Force of Vegetative and Animal Substances*.
> 
> ### 4. The Role of Living Matter
> 
> Humanity, as living matter, is inseparably linked to the material-energy processes of the Earth's geological envelope—the biosphere. It cannot physically exist independently of it for even a moment.
> 
> The term "biosphere" ("sphere of life") was introduced to biology by Lamarck (1744–1829) in the early 19th century in Paris and to geology by E. Suess (1831–1914) in Vienna at the end of the same century.
> 
> In our century, the biosphere has taken on a new meaning, emerging as a planetary phenomenon of cosmic character.
> 
> In biogeochemistry, we now recognize that life (living organisms) likely exists beyond our planet—not only in Earth's biosphere but potentially on other "terrestrial planets" such as Venus, Earth, and Mars.
> 
> ### 5. Cosmic Life and Biogeochemical Studies
> 
> In the Biogeochemical Laboratory of the Academy of Sciences in Moscow, now renamed the Laboratory of Geochemical Problems, in collaboration with the Institute of Microbiology (under the direction of Corresponding Member of the Academy of Sciences, B. L. Isachenko), we raised the question of cosmic life as a current scientific task in 1940.
> 
> This work was suspended due to wartime events and will resume at the first opportunity.
> 
> The idea of life as a cosmic phenomenon has existed in the archives of science for a long time. Centuries ago, at the end of the 17th century, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695) introduced this concept in his final work, *Cosmotheoros*, published posthumously.
> 
> This book was twice published in Russian at the initiative of Peter I under the title *Book of the Universe* in the first quarter of the 18th century.
> 
> Huygens scientifically generalized that "life is a cosmic phenomenon, distinct in some way from inert matter." I recently referred to this as "Huygens’ principle."
> 
> Living matter constitutes an insignificant fraction of the planet's mass. It seems this has been the case throughout geological time. It is concentrated in a thin, more or less continuous layer on the surface of the land in the troposphere—in forests and fields—and throughout the ocean. Its quantity is estimated at no more than fractions of a percent of the biosphere's mass, likely around 0.25%.
> 
> On land, it is not found in continuous formations at depths greater than 3 kilometers on average. Outside the biosphere, it does not exist.
> 
> Over geological time, living matter changes morphologically. The history of living matter over time is expressed in the slow transformation of life forms and living organisms, genetically connected and uninterrupted across generations.
> 
> ### 6. The Role of Living and Biogenic Matter
> 
> If the quantity of living matter appears negligible compared to the inert and biocosmic masses of the biosphere, biogenic rocks (i.e., those created by living matter) constitute a substantial portion of its mass and extend far beyond the boundaries of the biosphere.
> 
> Through the process of metamorphism, these rocks transform, losing all traces of life, into the Earth's granitic crust and thus exit the biosphere. The granitic crust of the Earth represents the remnants of past biospheres. In the remarkable 1802 work *Hydrogeology* by Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, living matter, as I understand it, was presented as the creator of the principal rock formations of our planet. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck de Monet (1744–1829), however, did not accept the discoveries of Antoine Lavoisier (1743–1794) until his death. Another prominent chemist, Jean-Baptiste Dumas (1800–1884), who extensively studied the chemistry of living matter, long adhered to the idea of the quantitative significance of living matter in the formation of the biosphere’s rocks.
> 
> ### 7. The Direction of Evolution and Geological Discoveries
> 
> The younger contemporaries of Charles Darwin—James Dwight Dana (1813–1895) and Joseph LeConte (1823–1901), two leading North American geologists (Dana was also a mineralogist and biologist)—identified empirical generalizations before 1859 that suggested the evolution of living matter progresses in a specific direction.
> 
> This phenomenon was termed "cephalization" by Dana and "the psychosoic era" by LeConte. Like Darwin, Dana arrived at these ideas and his understanding of living nature during his global voyage, which he began in 1838—two years after Darwin returned to London—and concluded in 1842.
> 
> It is worth noting that the expedition during which Dana reached his conclusions about cephalization, coral islands, and other phenomena was historically closely tied to Pacific Ocean research conducted by Russian sailors, primarily Ivan Kruzenshtern (1770–1846). Published in German, these expeditions inspired the American lawyer John Reynolds to advocate for organizing a similar American marine scientific expedition. Reynolds began his campaign in 1827, following the publication of Kruzenshtern’s expedition. Finally, in 1838, after eleven years of persistent effort, this expedition materialized as the Wilkes Expedition, which definitively proved the existence of Antarctica.
> 
> ### 8. Empirical Ideas on Evolutionary Processes
> 
> Empirical ideas about the directionality of evolutionary processes—without attempting to theoretically substantiate them—go back to the 18th century. Even Buffon (1707–1788) spoke of the reign of humanity, based on humanity’s geological significance.
> 
> The idea of evolution was foreign to Buffon, as it was to Louis Agassiz (1807–1873), who introduced the concept of the Ice Age into science. Agassiz lived in an era of rapid geological advancement. He considered the geological era of humanity to have begun but, influenced by theological views, opposed evolutionary theory. LeConte points out that Dana, who had initially held views similar to Agassiz, embraced the idea of evolution in its then-standard Darwinian understanding in his later years. The difference between LeConte’s "psychosoic era" and Dana’s "cephalization" disappeared.
> 
> Unfortunately, in our country especially, this significant empirical generalization remains outside the scope of biologists.
> 
> The validity of Dana’s principle (the psychosoic era of LeConte), which has eluded the attention of our paleontologists, can be easily verified by anyone willing to examine any modern paleontology course. It encompasses not only the entire animal kingdom but also manifests within individual types of animals.
> 
> Dana noted that over geological time—at least two billion years and likely much longer—there has been (in leaps) an improvement or growth of the central nervous system (the brain), beginning with crustaceans, where Dana empirically established his principle, and progressing through mollusks (cephalopods) to humans. This phenomenon, which Dana termed "cephalization," shows that once a level of brain development (of the central nervous system) is reached in evolution, it does not regress but only progresses further.
> 
> ### 9. Humanity as a Geological Force
> 
> Based on humanity’s geological role, A. P. Pavlov (1854–1929) spoke in the final years of his life about the Anthropogenic era, which we are now living through. He did not account for the spiritual and material destruction we are now experiencing as a result of the barbaric invasion of the Germans and their allies, just over ten years after his death. However, he correctly emphasized that humanity is becoming a powerful and ever-growing geological force.
> 
> This geological force developed over a long period, imperceptibly to humanity. It coincided with the material transformation of humanity’s position on our planet.
> 
> In the 20th century, for the first time in Earth’s history, humanity has come to understand and encompass the entire biosphere, completed the geographical map of planet Earth, and spread across its surface. Humanity has become a unified whole. There is no part of Earth where humanity could not live if necessary. This was vividly demonstrated by our residence on the floating ice of the North Pole in 1937–1938. Simultaneously, thanks to advanced technology and scientific achievements, including radio and television, humanity can instantly communicate with anyone anywhere on the planet. Air and land transport has reached speeds of several hundred kilometers per hour, and this progress continues.
> 
> All of this is the result of Dana’s cephalization (1856), the growth of the human brain, and the labor guided by it.
> 
> Economist L. Brentano vividly illustrated the planetary significance of this phenomenon. He calculated that if every person were given one square meter and stood side by side, the entire human population would not even occupy the area of the small Lake Constance on the border of Bavaria and Switzerland. The rest of Earth’s surface would remain empty of humans. Thus, all of humanity collectively represents an insignificant mass of the planet’s matter. Its power is not in its physical substance but in its brain, reason, and labor directed by that reason.
> 
> In the geological history of the biosphere, humanity has an immense future ahead, provided it understands this and does not use its reason and labor for self-destruction.
> 
> ### 10. The Unity and Equality of Humanity
> 
> The geological evolutionary process corresponds to the biological unity and equality of all people—Homo sapiens and its geological ancestors, such as Sinanthropus and others. Their descendants, whether of white, red, yellow, or black races, develop unceasingly among countless generations. This is a law of nature. All races interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
> 
> In historical competition, such as a war of the current scale, in the end, victory goes to those who follow this law. Violating the principle of unity among all humans as a law of nature cannot go unpunished. Here, I use the term "law of nature" as it increasingly appears in the physical-chemical sciences—as an empirically established generalization.
> 
> The historical process before our eyes is undergoing fundamental change. For the first time in human history, the interests of the masses—of all and each individual—and the free thought of individuals determine humanity’s life and represent its sense of justice. Humanity as a whole is becoming a powerful geological force. Before it, before its thought and labor, lies the task of transforming the biosphere in the interests of a freely thinking humanity as a unified whole.
> 
> This new state of the biosphere, which we are approaching without realizing it, is the "noosphere."
> 
> ### 11. The Concept of the Noosphere
> 
> In 1922–1923, during lectures at the Sorbonne in Paris, I adopted biogeochemical phenomena as the foundation of the biosphere. Part of these lectures was published in my book *Sketches of Geochemistry.*
> 
> Taking the biogeochemical foundation of the biosphere established by me as a starting point, the French mathematician and Bergsonian philosopher E. Le Roy introduced the concept of the "noosphere" in 1927 during his lectures at the Collège de France in Paris as the modern stage of the biosphere’s geological evolution. He emphasized that he arrived at this idea together with his friend, the eminent geologist and paleontologist Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, who is now working in China.
> 
> ### 12. The Noosphere as a New Geological Phenomenon
> 
> The noosphere is a new geological phenomenon on our planet. For the first time, humanity becomes the greatest geological force. Humanity can and must restructure its domain of life through labor and thought, fundamentally transforming it compared to what existed before. Increasingly broad creative possibilities lie ahead. Perhaps my granddaughter’s generation will witness their flowering.
> 
> A new enigma has arisen: thought is not a form of energy. How, then, can it alter material processes? This question remains scientifically unresolved. As far as I know, it was first posed by the American scientist, born in Lviv, mathematician and biophysicist Alfred Lotka. However, he could not solve it.
> 
> As Goethe (1749–1832)—not only a great poet but also a great scientist—once rightly said, in science, we can only know how something happened, not why or for what purpose.
> 
> Empirical results of this "incomprehensible" process are evident around us at every step.
> 
> Mineralogical rarities, such as native iron, are now produced in billions of tons. Never before existing on our planet, native aluminum is now manufactured in any quantity. The same applies to the nearly countless artificially created chemical compounds (cultural biogenic minerals). The mass of such artificial minerals continuously grows. All strategic raw materials fall into this category.
> 
> The face of the planet—the biosphere—is being chemically transformed by humanity, both consciously and, primarily, unconsciously. Humanity is also physically and chemically altering the Earth’s atmosphere and all its natural waters.
> 
> As human culture advanced in the 20th century, coastal seas and parts of the ocean began to change (chemically and biologically) more dramatically. Humanity must now take increasingly significant measures to preserve marine resources for future generations.
> 
> Moreover, humanity is creating new species and breeds of animals and plants.
> 
> Future possibilities seem like fairy-tale dreams: humanity aspires to venture beyond its planet into cosmic space. And, likely, it will succeed.
> 
> In the present, we cannot ignore that amidst the great historical tragedy we are experiencing, we have embarked on the correct path, which aligns with the noosphere.
> 
> Statesmen and historians are only beginning to approach the phenomena of nature from this perspective. Winston S. Churchill’s (1932) reflections on this issue, both as a historian and a statesman, are particularly intriguing.
> 
> ### 13. The Noosphere as the Current State of the Biosphere
> 
> The noosphere—the latest of many evolutionary states of the biosphere in geological history—is the state of our present era. The course of this process is only beginning to be understood through the study of its geological past in certain aspects.
> 
> Let me provide a few examples. Five hundred million years ago, during the Cambrian geological era, calcium-rich skeletal structures first appeared in the biosphere. These were structures of animals, whereas for plants, a similar transformation occurred over two billion years ago. This calcium function of living matter, now highly developed, represented one of the most significant evolutionary stages of the geological transformation of the biosphere.
> 
> Another critical change in the biosphere occurred 70–110 million years ago, during the Cretaceous and especially the Tertiary periods. During this time, our familiar green forests were created in the biosphere. These forests, deeply significant to all of us, represent another major evolutionary stage analogous to the noosphere. Likely, it was within these forests that humanity first appeared about 15–20 million years ago through evolutionary processes.
> 
> Today, we are experiencing a new geological evolutionary change in the biosphere. We are entering the noosphere.
> 
> We step into it—a new, spontaneous geological process—in a formidable time, during a destructive world war.
> 
> However, for us, it is crucial to recognize that the ideals of our democracy are in harmony with this spontaneous geological process and the laws of nature, aligning with the noosphere.
> 
> Thus, we can confidently look to our future—it is in our hands, and we will not let it slip away.
